Failure risk

    Ranking quality, and what a threshold is worth

    0.5 is a default, not an answer — at 0.5 this model flags nothing at all. The right chart sweeps every threshold against real money. No threshold on that curve was chosen from the test set.

    How the score becomes a decision key result

    Three ways to turn the same scores into flags, measured on the same held-out orders. None of them saw the test set before being defined.

    Against rules a merchant could apply without a model

    Each rule at its own natural operating point, over the whole held-out set ( orders).

    Same flag rate, model versus rule

    A rule flagging 60% of orders will always catch more failures than a model flagging 18%. Holding the flag rate equal isolates ranking quality from aggressiveness.

    Calibration

    Does a predicted risk of 20% mean 20 in 100 actually fail?

    What the score actually relies on

    Permutation importance on the test set — measured drop in held-out performance when a feature is shuffled.

    Does the finding survive at other dates? rolling backtest

    The whole experiment repeated at five cut points, each fitted only on its own past. The five test windows are disjoint, so they are five observations rather than one smeared across five.

    If the assumed costs are wrong sensitivity

    Freight is measured. Friction and prevention are judgement calls, and the policy is derived from them — so the sweep re-derives every decision at each point rather than re-pricing a fixed set of flags.

    Are the comparisons real, or noise? bootstrap

    600 resamples of the held-out set. An interval that crosses zero means a tie — not a win, and not a loss.

    Where it is weakest

    Averages hide the segments a model is systematically wrong about. Worst recall first; segments under 300 orders dropped as noise.

    Everything a reviewer needs to check the numbers above were earned rather than arranged.

    Limitations
